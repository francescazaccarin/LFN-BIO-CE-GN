\section{Introduction}

Gene interaction networks provide a graph-based representation of genetic relationships, where genes are modelled as nodes and interactions as edges. Analysing such networks with graph-theoretic tools can reveal structural patterns that are informative about candidate regulators, hub genes, and higher-level organisational structure.

In this project, we investigate the BIO-CE-GN dataset of genetic interactions in \textit{Caenorhabditis elegans} by constructing a gene interaction network and studying it through node-level centrality measures and correlation analysis. Specifically, we compute degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and PageRank to capture complementary notions of node importance, and we examine the correlation between these measures to understand how different centrality-based rankings agree or differ.

Beyond node-level properties, we also discuss community structure as a qualitative framework to interpret densely connected groups of genes as candidate functional modules. Overall, the goal is to provide an interpretable structural characterisation of the BIO-CE-GN interaction network using techniques introduced during the course, and to outline how these network-level findings can motivate biological follow-up analyses.

\section{Dataset and Network Construction}

The analysis is based on the BIO-CE-GN dataset of genetic interactions in \textit{Caenorhabditis elegans}. The data was visualized and shown in the form of a network of genes where nodes correspond to genes and edges correspond to interactions between genes. In this graph representation, it makes it easy to use the usual network analysis methods to characterize the structural behaviour of the interaction system.

Self-loops were dropped before preprocessing to avoid artificially inflating node centrality scores. The network was modelled as a simple undirected graph, which guarantees a uniform treatment of the centrality measures and supports comparison across metrics. This modeling decision adheres to the project instructions and is consistent with the network representations introduced in class.

The resulting network has 2,220 nodes and 53,683 edges (average degree = 48.36; density = 0.0218). The graph is comprised of three connected components which suggests that most genes are linked together well, but few nodes make up a small set of isolated subgraphs. The number of units in the network also influences its characteristics. A connectivity pattern indicates a predominantly integrated interaction network.

These fundamental network statistics, thus, illustrate the interaction structure globally, which will serve as the foundation for the centrality and correlation analysis discussed later in the page.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Centrality Analysis}

In biological interaction networks, various structural roles could be associated with differing biological hypotheses. Hub-like (high degree or PageRank) genes may be representative of those broadly interacting genes, and bridge-like (high betweenness) genes may indicate those genes that organize the interactions across modules. Hence, joint interpretation of centrality scores is more informative than considering a single measure alone.

To characterize the structural roles of individual genes in the BIO-CE-GN interaction network, we calculated three node-level centrality measures: degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and PageRank. There is a parallel set of nodes with values in different axes of centrality in the topology as reported in the above metrics.

Degree centrality indicates local connectivity by counting the number of direct interactions, or “hubs,” of a gene in the network. Genes that are central in a big way can be regarded as hubs and potentially involved in a wide variety of genetic interactions.

Betweenness centrality represents the fraction of shortest pathways in the network and shows genes that serve as a bridge between nodes. These nodes may also act as intermediaries that connect areas of interaction structure which are not very connected and help to facilitate connectivity in the network.

PageRank adds some global weight to the concept of importance by considering not only the number of connections between a gene but also the relevance of its neighbouring genes with a random walk. Consequently, genes with high PageRank are not necessarily the most important but rather the embedded genes in influential layers of the network.

All centrality scores were computed on the preprocessed undirected simple graph from the BIO-CE-GN edge list (self-loops removed). Degree centrality measured local connectivity, PageRank gave a global ranking depending on neighbourhood influence, and betweenness centrality reflected potential connector nodes that connect different areas of the network. Employing multiple measures lessens the bias of a single concept of importance, while giving a more reliable structural typology of candidate genes.

\section{Interpretation of Current Results}

The node-level analysis based on different centrality measures provides complementary insights into the structural roles of genes in the BIO-CE-GN interaction network. In particular, degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and PageRank capture different notions of node importance within the network.

As discussed in class, degree centrality reflects local connectivity by measuring how many direct interactions a gene has, while betweenness centrality highlights genes that act as bridges between different parts of the network. PageRank, instead, captures a notion of global importance by accounting not only for the number of connections, but also for the importance of neighbouring nodes from a random-walk perspective.

The correlation analysis further clarifies the relationship between these measures. The strong correlation observed between degree centrality and PageRank suggests that genes with many interactions also tend to be globally influential within the network. In contrast, betweenness centrality exhibits lower correlation with the other measures, indicating that it captures complementary structural information by identifying genes that play a mediating or connector role. These differences motivate a combined use of centrality measures to characterise gene importance from multiple structural perspectives.

\section{Community Detection and Assessment of Functional Relevance}

In addition to node-level centrality measures, the project also considers the mesoscopic organisation of the network through community detection. Communities are defined as groups of nodes that are more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the graph, and they provide insight into higher-level structural patterns that cannot be captured by individual node metrics alone.

Following the project guidelines, the analysis is restricted to network-based methods introduced during the course. In this context, community detection is performed using modularity-based approaches, which aim to identify partitions of the network that maximise intra-community connectivity while minimising inter-community links. These methods are commonly adopted in network science and are consistent with the techniques discussed in class.

The detected communities are interpreted as structural modules emerging from the topology of the gene interaction network. In biological networks, such modules are often considered candidate functional units, as genes that are densely interconnected may participate in related biological processes or pathways.

The correspondence between communities and known biological pathways is assessed at a qualitative and exploratory level. Rather than performing external biological enrichment analyses, which are beyond the scope of this course, the evaluation focuses on network-based properties such as the internal connectivity of communities and the presence of highly central genes within clusters. This approach provides an initial indication of the potential functional relevance of the detected communities while remaining fully consistent with the methodological constraints of the course.
